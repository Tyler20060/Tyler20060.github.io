# Types of Colour Blindness

Colour blindness comes in several forms, each affecting vision differently. Most people with colour blindness have red-green colour blindness, while blue-yellow and complete colour blindness are much rarer.

## Overview: The Main Types

<div class="types-grid">
  <div class="type-card">
    <h3>🔴🟢 Red-Green Colour Blindness</h3>
    <p><strong>Most Common Type</strong></p>
    <p>Difficulty distinguishing red from green</p>
    <p><strong>Affects:</strong> ~6% of males, 0.4% of females</p>
  </div>

  <div class="type-card">
    <h3>🔵🟡 Blue-Yellow Colour Blindness</h3>
    <p><strong>Rare Type</strong></p>
    <p>Difficulty distinguishing blue from yellow</p>
    <p><strong>Affects:</strong> ~0.001% equally in males and females</p>
  </div>

  <div class="type-card">
    <h3>⚪ Complete Colour Blindness</h3>
    <p><strong>Extremely Rare Type</strong></p>
    <p>Cannot perceive any color (grayscale vision)</p>
    <p><strong>Affects:</strong> ~0.003% equally in males and females</p>
  </div>
</div>

## Red-Green Colour Blindness

### The Most Common Form

Red-green colour blindness accounts for approximately **99% of all colour blindness cases**. It primarily affects males due to its X-linked inheritance pattern.

### Protanopia (Red-Blind)

<div class="card">
  <strong>🔴 Protanopia Characteristics:</strong><br><br>
  
  <strong>What It Means:</strong>
  Missing or non-functional long-wavelength (red) cones
  
  <strong>How It Affects Vision:</strong>
  <ul>
    <li>Red colors appear as dark brown or black</li>
    <li>Orange and yellow appear more yellow</li>
    <li>Green appears more bluish</li>
    <li>Overall vision may be slightly dimmer</li>
    <li>Can see approximately 500 hues (vs 10,000,000 in normal vision)</li>
  </ul>
  
  <strong>Frequency:</strong>
  <ul>
    <li>~1% of males</li>
    <li>~0.02% of females</li>
  </ul>
  
  <strong>Common Confusions:</strong>
  <ul>
    <li>Red ↔ Brown</li>
    <li>Red ↔ Black</li>
    <li>Green ↔ Blue</li>
    <li>Orange ↔ Yellow</li>
  </ul>
</div>

### Deuteranopia (Green-Blind)

<div class="card">
  <strong>🟢 Deuteranopia Characteristics:</strong><br><br>
  
  <strong>What It Means:</strong>
  Missing or non-functional medium-wavelength (green) cones
  
  <strong>How It Affects Vision:</strong>
  <ul>
    <li>Green colors appear more reddish or brownish</li>
    <li>Red appears more orange or brown</li>
    <li>Yellow appears more orange</li>
    <li>Overall color perception similar to protanopia</li>
    <li>Similar hue range as protanopia (~500 hues)</li>
  </ul>
  
  <strong>Frequency:</strong>
  <ul>
    <li>~1% of males</li>
    <li>~0.01% of females</li>
  </ul>
  
  <strong>Common Confusions:</strong>
  <ul>
    <li>Red ↔ Brown</li>
    <li>Red ↔ Orange</li>
    <li>Green ↔ Brown</li>
    <li>Yellow ↔ Pink</li>
  </ul>
</div>

### Anomalous Trichromacy (Mild Red-Green)

<div class="highlight-box">
  <strong>🟡 Anomalous Trichromacy:</strong><br><br>
  
  <strong>What It Means:</strong>
  The cones are present but their wavelength sensitivity is shifted (weak or shifted color sensitivity)
  
  <strong>Types:</strong>
  <ul>
    <li><strong>Protanomalous:</strong> Weak red vision</li>
    <li><strong>Deuteranomalous:</strong> Weak green vision (most common)</li>
  </ul>
  
  <strong>How It Affects Vision:</strong>
  <ul>
    <li>Milder than dichromacy (protanopia/deuteranopia)</li>
    <li>Can see more colors than dichromats (~10,000 hues)</li>
    <li>Still have difficulty with color discrimination</li>
    <li>May not notice their colour blindness in daily life</li>
  </ul>
  
  <strong>Frequency:</strong>
  <ul>
    <li>Most common form of colour blindness overall</li>
    <li>Affects ~4% of males, 0.3% of females</li>
  </ul>
</div>

## Blue-Yellow Colour Blindness

### Tritanopia (Blue-Yellow Blind)

<div class="card">
  <strong>🔵 Tritanopia Characteristics:</strong><br><br>
  
  <strong>What It Means:</strong>
  Missing or non-functional short-wavelength (blue) cones
  
  <strong>How It Affects Vision:</strong>
  <ul>
    <li>Blue appears more like pink or purple</li>
    <li>Yellow appears pinkish or light gray</li>
    <li>Can see red and green relatively well</li>
    <li>Main confusion is between blue and yellow/pink</li>
    <li>Brightness and contrast perception normal</li>
  </ul>
  
  <strong>Frequency:</strong>
  <ul>
    <li>Extremely rare</li>
    <li>~0.001% of population</li>
    <li>Affects males and females equally</li>
    <li>Often autosomal (not X-linked)</li>
  </ul>
  
  <strong>Common Confusions:</strong>
  <ul>
    <li>Blue ↔ Purple</li>
    <li>Blue ↔ Pink</li>
    <li>Yellow ↔ Pink</li>
    <li>Blue ↔ Gray</li>
  </ul>
</div>

### Tritanomaly (Weak Blue-Yellow Vision)

<div class="highlight-box">
  <strong>🟢 Tritanomaly Characteristics:</strong><br><br>
  
  <strong>What It Means:</strong>
  S-cones present but with shifted wavelength sensitivity
  
  <strong>How It Affects Vision:</strong>
  <ul>
    <li>Milder version of tritanopia</li>
    <li>Subtle difficulty with blue-yellow discrimination</li>
    <li>May go unnoticed in daily life</li>
    <li>Can see more blue-yellow variations than tritanopia</li>
  </ul>
  
  <strong>Frequency:</strong>
  <ul>
    <li>Even rarer than tritanopia</li>
    <li>Affects males and females equally</li>
  </ul>
</div>

## Complete Colour Blindness (Achromatopsia/Monochromacy)

### Monochromacy: Seeing in Black and White

<div class="card">
  <strong>⚪ Achromatopsia Characteristics:</strong><br><br>
  
  <strong>What It Means:</strong>
  Absence of all three types of color cones OR all cones are non-functional
  
  <strong>How It Affects Vision:</strong>
  <ul>
    <li>Complete absence of color perception</li>
    <li>Vision is entirely grayscale (black, white, grays)</li>
    <li>All colors appear as shades of gray</li>
    <li>Often accompanied by other vision problems:</li>
    <ul>
      <li>Nystagmus (involuntary eye movements)</li>
      <li>Photophobia (light sensitivity)</li>
      <li>Reduced visual acuity</li>
    </ul>
    <li>Daily challenges are more significant than other types</li>
  </ul>
  
  <strong>Types:</strong>
  <ul>
    <li><strong>Rod monochromacy:</strong> Only rod cells function (see in dim light well)</li>
    <li><strong>Cone monochromacy:</strong> Only one type of cone function (rare)</li>
  </ul>
  
  <strong>Frequency:</strong>
  <ul>
    <li>Extremely rare</li>
    <li>~0.003% of population (fewer than 1 in 30,000)</li>
    <li>Affects males and females equally</li>
    <li>Usually autosomal recessive inheritance</li>
  </ul>
  
  <strong>Famous Examples:</strong>
  <ul>
    <li>Oliver Sacks documented cases of achromatopsia</li>
    <li>Blind filmmaker Pete Sackson</li>
    <li>Various athletes and professionals manage with achromatopsia</li>
  </ul>
</div>

### Living with Achromatopsia

<div class="info-box">
  <strong>🌍 Adaptation Strategies:</strong><br><br>
  
  <strong>Daily Challenges:</strong>
  <ul>
    <li>Objects distinguished only by brightness/shape</li>
    <li>Difficulty in bright light (photophobia)</li>
    <li>May require special lenses or sunglasses</li>
    <li>Need clear labels and high contrast</li>
  </ul>
  
  <strong>Helpful Adaptations:</strong>
  <ul>
    <li>High-contrast labels and markers</li>
    <li>Protective eyewear for bright environments</li>
    <li>Good lighting control (not too bright or dim)</li>
    <li>Shape, texture, and position for identification</li>
    <li>Assistive technology and screen readers</li>
  </ul>
  
  <strong>Support Resources:</strong>
  <ul>
    <li>Low-vision services and rehabilitation</li>
    <li>Specialized training programs</li>
    <li>Community organizations for achromatopsia</li>
    <li>Vision rehabilitation therapists</li>
  </ul>
</div>

## Acquired Colour Blindness

### Colour Blindness Caused by Illness or Injury

<div class="warning-box">
  <strong>⚠️ Acquired Colour Blindness:</strong><br><br>
  
  <strong>Causes:</strong>
  <ul>
    <li><strong>Eye Disease:</strong> Macular degeneration, cataracts, retinitis pigmentosa</li>
    <li><strong>Neurological:</strong> Stroke, brain injury, Parkinson's disease</li>
    <li><strong>Medication:</strong> Some medications affect color perception</li>
    <li><strong>Chemical Exposure:</strong> Occupational exposure to toxins</li>
    <li><strong>Diabetes:</strong> Diabetic retinopathy can affect color vision</li>
  </ul>
  
  <strong>Characteristics:</strong>
  <ul>
    <li>Develops later in life (not from birth)</li>
    <li>May be progressive or sudden</li>
    <li>Often accompanied by other vision changes</li>
    <li>Sometimes reversible if underlying cause is treated</li>
  </ul>
  
  <strong>When to See a Doctor:</strong>
  <ul>
    <li>Sudden color vision changes</li>
    <li>Progressive color blindness</li>
    <li>Color blindness with other vision problems</li>
    <li>New color difficulties after medication start</li>
  </ul>
</div>

## Testing and Diagnosis

### Ishihara Colour Blindness Test

<div class="card">
  <strong>🔍 How Color Vision is Tested:</strong><br><br>
  
  <strong>Ishihara Test (Most Common):</strong>
  <ul>
    <li>Plates with colored dots</li>
    <li>Numbers hidden in the dots</li>
    <li>Colour-blind people see different numbers (or none)</li>
    <li>Quick and simple screening test</li>
  </ul>
  
  <strong>Other Tests:</strong>
  <ul>
    <li><strong>Farnsworth-Munsell Test:</strong> Arrange color caps by hue</li>
    <li><strong>Nagel Anomaloscope:</strong> Clinical standard (expensive)</li>
    <li><strong>Chromosome Testing:</strong> Genetic confirmation</li>
    <li><strong>Visual Evoked Potentials:</strong> Measures electrical brain response</li>
  </ul>
</div>

### When to Get Tested

<div class="highlight-box">
  <strong>📋 Reasons to Test:</strong><br><br>
  
  <ul>
    <li>Family history of colour blindness</li>
    <li>Suspected color vision problems</li>
    <li>Career requirements (aviation, military, etc.)</li>
    <li>Acquired color vision changes</li>
    <li>School or workplace accommodations</li>
    <li>Medical diagnosis of eye disease</li>
  </ul>
</div>

## Key Takeaways

✅ **Red-green colour blindness** is by far the most common type (99% of cases)

✅ **Blue-yellow and complete** colour blindness are extremely rare

✅ **Most colour blindness is genetic** and present from birth

✅ **Some colour blindness** develops later due to illness or injury

✅ **Testing is simple** with multiple options available

✅ **The type matters** for daily challenges and accommodations needed

✅ **Each person experiences** their specific type differently
