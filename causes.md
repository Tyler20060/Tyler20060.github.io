# Causes & Genetics of Colour Blindness

Most colour blindness is inherited through genes. Understanding the genetic basis helps explain why it's more common in men and how it runs in families.

## The Biology of Color Vision

### How Eyes Detect Color

The human eye contains millions of light-sensitive cells called photoreceptors. There are two main types:

<div class="card">
  <strong>📍 Rods and Cones:</strong><br><br>
  
  <strong>Rods (Rod Cells):</strong>
  <ul>
    <li>About 120 million per eye</li>
    <li>Detect light and dark (grayscale)</li>
    <li>Very sensitive to low light</li>
    <li>Responsible for night vision</li>
    <li>Color-blind (no color perception)</li>
  </ul>
  
  <strong>Cones (Cone Cells):</strong>
  <ul>
    <li>About 6-7 million per eye</li>
    <li>Detect color</li>
    <li>Concentrated in the macula (center of vision)</li>
    <li>Three types, each sensitive to different wavelengths</li>
    <li>Essential for color vision</li>
  </ul>
</div>

### The Three Types of Cones

People with normal color vision have three types of cone cells:

<div class="types-grid">
  <div class="type-card">
    <h3>S-Cones (Short)</h3>
    <p><strong>Color:</strong> Blue</p>
    <p><strong>Wavelength:</strong> 420 nm (short waves)</p>
    <p><strong>Sensitivity:</strong> Violet and blue light</p>
    <p><strong>Function:</strong> Detect blue colors</p>
    <p><strong>Proportion:</strong> 2-3% of all cones</p>
  </div>

  <div class="type-card">
    <h3>M-Cones (Medium)</h3>
    <p><strong>Color:</strong> Green</p>
    <p><strong>Wavelength:</strong> 530 nm (medium waves)</p>
    <p><strong>Sensitivity:</strong> Green light</p>
    <p><strong>Function:</strong> Detect green colors</p>
    <p><strong>Proportion:</strong> 30-40% of all cones</p>
  </div>

  <div class="type-card">
    <h3>L-Cones (Long)</h3>
    <p><strong>Color:</strong> Red</p>
    <p><strong>Wavelength:</strong> 560 nm (long waves)</p>
    <p><strong>Sensitivity:</strong> Red light</p>
    <p><strong>Function:</strong> Detect red colors</p>
    <p><strong>Proportion:</strong> 60-70% of all cones</p>
  </div>
</div>

### How Color Perception Works

<div class="highlight-box">
  <strong>The Process:</strong>
  <ol>
    <li>Light enters the eye and hits the retina</li>
    <li>Cone cells contain light-sensitive proteins (opsins)</li>
    <li>Different cone types absorb different light wavelengths</li>
    <li>Each cone type sends a signal to the brain</li>
    <li>The brain compares signals from all three cone types</li>
    <li>Brain interprets the combination as a specific color</li>
  </ol>
</div>

## Genetic Inheritance

### X-Linked Inheritance (Red-Green)

Red-green colour blindness follows an **X-linked recessive inheritance pattern**. This is why it's more common in men:

<div class="card">
  <strong>🧬 Why Men Are More Affected:</strong><br><br>
  
  <strong>Chromosome Basics:</strong>
  <ul>
    <li><strong>Men:</strong> XY chromosomes (one X, one Y)</li>
    <li><strong>Women:</strong> XX chromosomes (two X chromosomes)</li>
    <li>The colour blindness gene is on the X chromosome</li>
  </ul>
  
  <strong>The Math:</strong>
  <ul>
    <li><strong>Man with gene:</strong> X^c Y = Colour blind (only one X, so gene is expressed)</li>
    <li><strong>Woman with gene:</strong> X^c X = Carrier (other X chromosome is normal)</li>
    <li><strong>Woman to be colour blind:</strong> X^c X^c = Needs gene on BOTH X chromosomes (very rare)</li>
  </ul>
</div>

### Inheritance Patterns

<div class="info-box">
  <strong>Probability of Inheritance:</strong><br><br>
  
  If <strong>mother is a carrier</strong> (X^C X^c):
  <ul>
    <li>50% chance sons will be colour blind</li>
    <li>50% chance daughters will be carriers</li>
  </ul>
  
  If <strong>father is colour blind</strong> (X^c Y):
  <ul>
    <li>All daughters will be carriers (inherit father's X^c)</li>
    <li>All sons will have normal vision (inherit X^C from mother)</li>
  </ul>
  
  If <strong>both parents carry/have the gene</strong> (rare):
  <ul>
    <li>Daughters could be colour blind</li>
    <li>All sons of colour-blind mother will be colour blind</li>
  </ul>
</div>

### Punnett Square Example

<div class="card">
  <strong>Example: Carrier Mother × Normal Father</strong><br><br>
  
  Mother: X^C X^c (carrier, normal vision)
  Father: X^C Y (normal vision)
  
  ```
           X^C (Father)    Y (Father)
  X^C      X^C X^C         X^C Y
  (Mom)    Normal daughter Normal son
  
  X^c      X^C X^c         X^c Y
  (Mom)    Carrier daughter Colour-blind son
  ```
  
  <strong>Result:</strong> 50% chance of colour-blind sons, 50% chance of carrier daughters
</div>

## Genetic Mutations

### The Genes Involved

<div class="highlight-box">
  <strong>Genes Causing Colour Blindness:</strong><br><br>
  
  <strong>Red-Green Colour Blindness:</strong>
  <ul>
    <li><strong>OPN1LW gene:</strong> Controls L-cones (red perception)</li>
    <li><strong>OPN1MW gene:</strong> Controls M-cones (green perception)</li>
    <li>Both located on X chromosome</li>
    <li>Very similar genes, mutations affect color perception</li>
  </ul>
  
  <strong>Blue-Yellow Colour Blindness:</strong>
  <ul>
    <li><strong>OPN1SW gene:</strong> Controls S-cones (blue perception)</li>
    <li>Located on chromosome 7 (autosomal)</li>
    <li>Rare mutations cause tritanopia</li>
  </ul>
  
  <strong>Complete Colour Blindness:</strong>
  <ul>
    <li><strong>CNGB3 gene:</strong> Most common cause</li>
    <li>Inherited as recessive on chromosome 8</li>
    <li>Other genes (GNAT2, PDE6C) can also cause it</li>
  </ul>
</div>

### Types of Mutations

<div class="card">
  <strong>🔬 How Mutations Cause Colour Blindness:</strong><br><br>
  
  <strong>Gene Deletion:</strong> Part or all of a cone gene is missing
  
  <strong>Gene Mutation:</strong> A change in the DNA code affects protein function
  
  <strong>Gene Disruption:</strong> Abnormal crossing over between L and M gene copies
  
  <strong>Loss of Function:</strong> Protein doesn't work properly to detect light
</div>

## Genetic Testing

### How It Works

<div class="info-box">
  <strong>Genetic Testing for Colour Blindness:</strong><br><br>
  
  <strong>DNA Analysis:</strong>
  <ul>
    <li>Blood sample or saliva collection</li>
    <li>DNA extracted from cells</li>
    <li>Specific genes sequenced and analyzed</li>
    <li>Mutations identified and documented</li>
  </ul>
  
  <strong>What It Shows:</strong>
  <ul>
    <li>Confirms genetic colour blindness diagnosis</li>
    <li>Identifies specific gene mutations</li>
    <li>Determines inheritance pattern</li>
    <li>Helps with family planning decisions</li>
  </ul>
</div>

### Who Should Get Tested?

<div class="warning-box">
  <strong>Testing May Be Recommended For:</strong>
  <ul>
    <li>People with suspected colour blindness</li>
    <li>Family members of colour-blind individuals</li>
    <li>Carrier women considering having children</li>
    <li>People in occupations requiring color vision</li>
    <li>Couples with family history of colour blindness</li>
  </ul>
</div>

## Non-Genetic Causes

Not all colour blindness is inherited:

<div class="card">
  <strong>⚠️ Acquired Colour Blindness:</strong><br><br>
  
  <strong>Eye Diseases:</strong>
  <ul>
    <li>Macular degeneration</li>
    <li>Glaucoma</li>
    <li>Diabetic retinopathy</li>
    <li>Cataracts</li>
  </ul>
  
  <strong>Medications:</strong>
  <ul>
    <li>Certain antibiotics</li>
    <li>Chemotherapy drugs</li>
    <li>Antiretroviral medications</li>
  </ul>
  
  <strong>Environmental Factors:</strong>
  <ul>
    <li>Toxic chemical exposure</li>
    <li>Aging process (presbyopia)</li>
    <li>Head injury or concussion</li>
    <li>Stroke or neurological damage</li>
  </ul>
</div>

## Evolutionary Perspective

### Why Do Humans Have Three Cone Types?

<div class="highlight-box">
  <strong>Evolution of Color Vision:</strong><br><br>
  
  Most mammals have only two types of cones (red and green). Primates evolved a third cone type (blue) for better color discrimination.
  
  <strong>Benefits of Trichromacy:</strong>
  <ul>
    <li>Better detection of ripe fruit (reds and yellows)</li>
    <li>Easier spotting of prey in foliage</li>
    <li>Improved visual discrimination in general</li>
    <li>Evolutionary advantage in finding food</li>
  </ul>
  
  This is why humans with normal vision have a significant advantage in color-dependent tasks compared to people with colour blindness.
</div>

### Genetic Variation

<div class="info-box">
  <strong>Interesting Facts:</strong><br><br>
  
  • <strong>Some women have 4 cone types!</strong> Called "tetrachromats," they can see even more colors than typical humans
  
  • <strong>Some birds have 5+ cone types</strong> and see colors we can't imagine
  
  • <strong>The genes are ancient</strong> - L and M cones evolved millions of years ago
  
  • <strong>Genetic diversity is normal</strong> - color blindness is just natural variation
</div>

## Key Takeaways

✅ **Color blindness is genetic** in most cases, caused by missing or faulty cone genes

✅ **Red-green is X-linked** - why men are more commonly affected

✅ **Two genes (OPN1LW and OPN1MW)** cause red-green colour blindness

✅ **Blue-yellow and complete** colour blindness follow different inheritance patterns

✅ **Genetic testing** can confirm diagnosis and identify specific mutations

✅ **Some colour blindness is acquired** - not all is genetic

✅ **Family planning** can be informed by genetic counseling
